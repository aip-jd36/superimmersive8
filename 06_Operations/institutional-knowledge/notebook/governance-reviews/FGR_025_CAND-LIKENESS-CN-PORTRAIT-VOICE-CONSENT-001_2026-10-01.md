**(1) Art. 990, full text, independently re-read:**

> 第九百九十条　人格权是民事主体享有的生命权、身体权、健康权、姓名权、名称权、肖像权、名誉权、荣誉权、隐私权等权利。

*("Art. 990. Personality rights are rights enjoyed by civil subjects, including the right to life, the right to bodily integrity, the right to health, the right to a name, the right to an entity name, the portrait right, the right to reputation, the right to honor, the right to privacy, and other such rights. **Finding:** Art. 990 is a flat, co-equal **enumeration** of named personality rights — "姓名权" (name right) and "肖像权" (portrait right) are listed as two **separate, independently named rights**, not as one undifferentiated "likeness" right and not in any stated hierarchy or subsumption relationship. Nothing in Art. 990's own text merges them. This directly bears on Phase 2 (claim granularity, §6 below): it is **textually incorrect** to describe Chinese law as protecting one unified "likeness" interest encompassing name — the Code itself keeps them as distinct named rights from the first article of the chapter.

## 2. Phase 2 — Governed subject, tested fresh (Alternatives A–F)

Independently tested, not inherited:

- **(C) AI-specific likeness proposition** — rejected. The Civil Code provisions (2020/2021) predate any AI-specific drafting by five years and are media/technology-agnostic on their own terms (Art. 1018 para. 2: "通过影像、雕塑、绘画等方式" — "via imagery, sculpture, painting, **or other means**" — an open-ended list). Drafting an "AI-specific" proposition would misrepresent a general rule as a special one.
- **(D)/(E) separate face-swap / separate voice-cloning propositions** — rejected. SPC 2026 Opinion Art. 4 treats AI face-swap and AI voice-cloning as **two instances of one underlying principle** (apply the existing name/portrait/voice statutory concepts to a new, AI-generated fact pattern), in one article, one sentence structure each, with no suggestion they require separate governed treatment. Splitting them would fragment one coherent judicial-policy statement without evidentiary basis.
- **(F) generic identity/personality-right proposition** — rejected outright, per this candidate's own explicit prohibition against importing a U.S.-style "right of publicity" or Lanham-style concept. China's own Civil Code keeps portrait, name, reputation, honor, and privacy as **separately enumerated** rights (Art. 990, §1 above) — a generic collapsed "personality right" proposition would misstate this structure.
- **(A) Civil-Code-level portrait+voice** — the strongest candidate, but tested, not assumed. §1's Art. 1023 finding (two different, narrower cross-references, not one blanket rule) means a **literal, unmodified "Option A"** that silently also absorbed name would be wrong. **Independent refinement, found by this review and not present in the Pre-FGR's own enumerated options:** name is excluded not merely for parsimony but because its own **operative prohibited-conduct standard is materially different**. Art. 1014 (Chapter 3, Name/Entity-Name Right): *"任何组织或者个人不得以干涉、盗用、假冒等方式侵害他人的姓名权或者名称权"* ("No organization or individual may infringe another's name right or entity-name right by **interference, misappropriation, or passing-off/impersonation**, or other such means") — this is a wrongful-conduct standard (interference/misappropriation/impersonation), **not** a blanket "cannot use without consent" rule. It is categorically different from Art. 1019's portrait-right standard (*any* unauthorized production/use/disclosure is prohibited, consent-gated, subject only to Art. 1020's five exceptions). Bundling name into this candidate's proposition under the Art. 1019/1020 consent-and-exception framework would misstate name right's own, narrower, fault/wrongful-conduct-shaped test. **Finding, independently derived, not inherited:** the correctly-scoped "Option A" is **portrait + voice only**, excluding name — supported by both the Art. 1023-structural finding (§1(3)) and this newly-surfaced Art. 1014 standard-mismatch finding.

**Disposition: governed subject = Civil Code portrait right (Arts. 1018–1020) + voice protection via Art. 1023 paragraph 2's reference-application of the same provisions, confirmed applicable to AI-generated/processed content by SPC 2026 Opinion Art. 4. Name right (Arts. 1012–1017, Art. 1023 paragraph 1) is explicitly excluded, for an evidenced, article-level reason, not asserted by fiat.**

**Draft governed proposition (not yet PM-approved wording):**

> Under PRC Civil Code (2020, effective 2021) Arts. 1018–1020, a natural person has a statutory portrait right over their own recognizable image — "the recognizable external image of a specific natural person reflected, by means of photography, sculpture, painting, or other methods, on a given medium" (Art. 1018) — and the right to decide whether and how that image is produced, used, or publicly disclosed. Art. 1019 prohibits defacing, defiling, or using information-technology means to forge a person's portrait, and prohibits producing, using, or publicly disclosing a person's portrait without that person's consent, except as otherwise provided by law. Art. 1020 lists five specific, narrow circumstances — personal study/artistic appreciation/classroom teaching/scientific-research use of an already-published portrait within a necessary scope; unavoidable use for news reporting; a state organ's lawful performance of duties within a necessary scope; unavoidable use to depict a specific public environment; and other acts to safeguard the public interest or the portrait-right holder's own lawful rights/interests — in which a portrait may be used without consent; this proposition does not determine whether any of these applies to a specific project. By Art. 1023 paragraph 2, protection of a natural person's voice applies, by reference, these same portrait-right protection provisions; Chinese courts applying this provision have required that the voice be recognizable as that specific person's own, based on characteristics such as timbre, tone, and pronunciation style, before protection attaches. China's Supreme People's Court 2026 Opinion on AI-related disputes, Article 4, confirms that these existing name-right, portrait-right, and voice-interest protections apply where generative AI is used, without the person's consent, to process a natural person's name or portrait, or to use their voice as training material, so as to generate an identifiable synthetic digital image or synthesized voice — applying the existing statutory concepts to a new technological fact pattern, not creating a new right. Infringement of these rights can expose the infringer to civil liability under Arts. 991, 995, and 1000, including an order to cease the infringing conduct and eliminate its effects, and — proportionate to the manner and scope of the infringing conduct — issue an apology; the right to demand cessation of infringement is not subject to a limitations period. This proposition addresses portrait right and voice protection only; it does not address China's separate right to a name (Arts. 1012–1017), which is governed by a materially different interference/misappropriation/impersonation standard, not a consent-based prohibition.

This wording: states recognizability as part of the statute's/case law's own definition (not a separate hedge, and not a dependency — see §7); names all five Art. 1020 exceptions without resolving any of them; states the portrait/voice relationship as reference-application; cites the SPC Opinion for the AI-specific confirmation without implying a new right; explicitly scopes out name right with its own stated reason; imports no "right of publicity," Lanham, NY-commercial-use, or U.S. fair-use terminology anywhere.

### 7.1 The recognizability bind — resolved, not merely restated

Both portrait (Art. 1018: "可以被识别的外部形象" — a recognizable external image) and voice (the Yin case's own holding, Load-bearing finding 6: recognizability "based on others' repeated or long-term listening," judged by timbre/tone/pronunciation style) build recognizability into the **statutory/judicial definition of the protected interest itself** — this is, if anything, more central to the Chinese framework than NY's own now-excised Loftus gloss (which was a secondary characterization layered on top of a statute that does not itself define "portrait" by recognizability). That makes recognizability **more**, not less, important to state faithfully in the proposition's own wording (done, §3) — and simultaneously makes it a **worse** candidate for a runtime dependency, for a structural reason independent of evidence quality:

- **Bundled** ("portrait-or-voice recognizability present"): fails prong (a). Visual recognizability (an image-identification judgment) and acoustic recognizability (a timbre/tone/pronunciation-pattern judgment, the Yin case's own distinct test) are two categorically different evaluative methods — the same class of defect `FGR_024` §10-REVISED-2 found fatal for bundling mark-similarity and goods-similarity into one Trademark dependency, applied here to two different *sensory* evaluative methods instead of two different *comparative* ones.
- **Split** (`portrait_recognizability_present` / `voice_recognizability_present`): fails prong (b). Because the governed proposition is unified (one Art. 1019/1020 rule, applied by reference to two modalities), and `unresolved_project_dependencies` cannot be scoped to "only when portrait-type content exists" vs. "only when voice-type content exists," a project containing only voice content would still show an unresolved `portrait_recognizability_present` flag that has no bearing on that project at all — the identical branch-inaccuracy defect `FGR_024` §10-REVISED found fatal for a Trademark dependency present in only two of three statutory items.

**No conditional/scoped dependency orchestration is invented to solve this** — the architecture does not support it (confirmed, §0 citations), and inventing one here would itself be a Hard Stop requiring separate architecture review (§16). **Resolution, as instructed: recognizability is not represented as a runtime dependency at all.** It lives in two places instead: the proposition's own wording (already stated, §3, as the statute's/case law's own definitional language — not softened, not removed, unlike NY's unsupported Loftus sentence) and Commercial Assurance's Domain L boundary (§15 below — "The 'identifiable' threshold... is a visual judgment call. ... The reviewer is not making a legal determination," directly on point and independently re-read, not merely cited).

### 7.2 Content-presence gate — tested and admitted

A distinct, prior question from recognizability: does the project's content depict/use this specific real person's portrait or voice **at all**? This is NY's own `recognizable_likeness_or_voice_present` dependency in substance (its own inline comment in `GOVERNED-CLAIMS.md`, independently re-read, describes it as "whether the specific project's content actually uses the person's name/portrait/picture/likeness/voice at all" — a presence question, despite its recognizability-flavored name, not a recognizability-threshold question). Re-tested fresh, not inherited:

- (a) **Statutory-clause-mirror, re-examined for the bundling concern that killed Trademark's D1:** is bundling "portrait content present OR voice content present" into one string the same disqualifying composite as bundling two *comparative evaluative methods*? **No — a materially different kind of bundling.** Trademark's rejected composite bundled two **evaluative comparison methods with branch-dependent thresholds** (identity required on one axis for item 1, mere similarity tolerated on another axis for item 3). A content-presence gate bundles two **simple existence checks** (does X appear at all), feeding into **one identical consequence regardless of which is true** — because the operative rule (Art. 1019/1020, extended to voice by Art. 1023¶2) is the *same* rule for both modalities, unlike Trademark's branch-varying thresholds. NY's own `recognizable_likeness_or_voice_present` dependency bundles *five* named statutory objects (name/portrait/picture/likeness/voice) this exact way, and was not challenged on bundling grounds by `FGR_008` or any later review. This review independently reaches the same conclusion for a narrower, two-object version: a pure disjunctive existence gate with one uniform consequence is not the kind of composite the admission test is built to catch.
- (b) **Claim-wide accuracy:** yes — because the proposition's own consequence (potential relevance) is identical whether portrait or voice content is present, a single "does either appear" gate is accurate across the whole claim, unlike the asymmetric recognizability case above.
- (c)-(j), applied: not a legal conclusion (closer to a plain observable fact than mark/goods comparison); no `ProjectFacts` field or resolution mechanism exists (same permanent-array-only status as every other dependency); CRC cannot establish it from conversation alone with confidence sufficient to resolve it, and per Domain L (§15, directly on point) a submitter's own bare assertion that "no real person appears" is explicitly **weak evidence** absent independent content review — this argues for evidence-only treatment (not askability), not against admitting the dependency; uniform and coherent as one gate (§(a)-(b) above); truthful, reviewer-facing language is straightforward ("whether this project's content depicts/uses this specific person's portrait or voice at all"); not self-attestable with confidence (Domain L's own "weak evidence" framing); does not ask CRC to resolve something reserved for Commercial Assurance — it flags exactly what Domain L's own stated objective is ("Through direct content review, determine whether any identifiable real person appears"), deferring to it rather than competing with it.

**Admitted: ONE dependency, `portrait_or_voice_content_present`, evidence-only.**
