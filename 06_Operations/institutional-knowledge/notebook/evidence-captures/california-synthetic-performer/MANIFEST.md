## What the raw capture establishes (quoted, not summarized)

- **Enactment, independently re-confirmed from two separate primary pages:** the chaptered bill text itself states, in a bracketed enactment block: *"Approved by Governor September 16, 2026. Filed with Secretary of State September 16, 2026."* and carries the heading *"CHAPTER 246"*. The separate bill-history page independently states the same two facts in its action table: *"09/16/26 — Chaptered by Secretary of State. Chapter 246, Statutes of 2026."* and *"09/16/26 — Approved by the Governor."* Two independent pages agree exactly — no discrepancy found.
- **Enacting clause (SEC. 2):** *"Article 10 (commencing with Section 17610) is added to Chapter 1 of Part 3 of Division 7 of the Business and Professions Code, to read: Article 10. Synthetic Performers"*
- **§ 17610(a)(1) "Advertisement":** *"any audio, video, or audiovisual message, statement, audiovisual recording, digital communication, or other representation disseminated in any manner or by any means, including through online platforms, that is intended to induce, or that is reasonably expected to induce, the purchase of goods or services, as described in Section 17500."*
- **§ 17610(a)(2)(A)-(B) "Advertising medium":** *"any broadcast station, cable operator, multichannel video programming distributor, online platform, streaming service, digital advertising network, publisher, or other person or entity that distributes, displays, transmits, or makes available an audio or audiovisual advertisement to consumers in this state."* Excludes *"a person or entity that is not capable of removing, disabling access to, or ceasing further dissemination of, an advertisement."*
- **§ 17610(a)(3) "Clear and conspicuous disclosure":** *"a disclosure that is difficult to miss, easily understandable, and presented in a manner that a reasonable consumer would notice, read, and comprehend, taking into account the medium, format, and context in which the advertisement appears."*
- **§ 17610(a)(4) "Generative artificial intelligence":** *"an artificial intelligence system that can generate derived synthetic content, such as text, images, video, and audio, that emulates the structure and characteristics of the system's training data."*
- **§ 17610(a)(5) "Prominently" — the three-part disjunctive test:** *"(A) In the foreground and demonstrating or illustrating the product or service. (B) Providing or voicing the on- or off-camera narration or commercial message. (C) Illustrating or reacting to the on- or off-camera narration or commercial message."*
- **§ 17610(a)(6) "Synthetic performer":** *"a digital figure, voice, or representation created in whole or in part using generative artificial intelligence that creates the realistic impression of the audio, audiovisual, or visual performance of a human performer who is not recognizable as any identifiable natural person."*
- **§ 17610(b) — the operative duty:** *"It is unlawful for any person to create and cause to be published in an advertising medium an advertisement that prominently includes a synthetic performer without a clear and conspicuous disclosure that the advertisement includes a synthetic performer."* **No actual-knowledge qualifier anywhere in this clause or elsewhere in § 17610** — confirmed by direct re-reading of the full captured text; contrast NY GBL § 396-b(3)'s express "where such person has actual knowledge."
- **§ 17610(c) — required disclosure wording:** *"substantially similar to 'this performance features a synthetic performer' or 'no human performer is depicted.'"*
- **§ 17610(d) — four "shall not be construed to" carve-outs:** (1) does not restrict/prohibit creation, distribution, or exhibition of synthetic content generally; (2) does not regulate expressive/informational ad content beyond the disclosure duty; (3) does not affect rights/remedies under §§ 17200/17500/other deceptive-practices law; (4) **does not apply to advertisements for expressive works** (motion pictures, TV, streaming, documentaries, video games, similar works), *"provided that the use of a synthetic performer in the advertisement or promotional material is consistent with its use in the expressive work."*
- **§ 17610(d)(5) — translation/accessibility exemption, broader than NY's:** *"Apply to an advertisement in which the use of the generative artificial intelligence solely involves the language translation of a human performer or other accessibility features."* (NY's own translation exemption, by contrast — captured separately, `evidence-captures/ny-general-business-law/MANIFEST.md` — covers only "language translation of a human performer," with no "or other accessibility features" clause.)
- **§ 17610(e) — the conditional, court-order-gated platform/medium duty (the major structural divergence from NY's unconditional platform exemption):** an advertising medium "shall not transmit, distribute, display, air, or otherwise make available" a non-compliant advertisement **only if both** (A) "a court of competent jurisdiction has issued an order finding that the advertisement violates this chapter or enjoining the creator from publishing the advertisement" **and** (B) "the advertising medium is served with the order and information reasonably sufficient to identify the advertisement." On receipt, the medium must, "as soon as commercially reasonable and technically feasible," remove/disable/cease dissemination "in this state" and cease accepting payment for further dissemination.
- **§ 17610(f) — enforcement cross-reference:** *"A violation of this section constitutes a violation of Section 17500 and may be enforced pursuant to Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 17200) of Part 2."* Confirmed directly against the separately captured §§ 17500/17200 text: § 17500 is a misdemeanor false-advertising provision (up to 6 months imprisonment and/or a $2,500 fine); § 17200 defines "unfair competition" to include "any act prohibited by Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 17500)" and is enforced under Chapter 5's remedies (injunctive relief, civil penalties, restitution — not independently re-read from Chapter 5's own further sections in this capture; the definitional cross-reference itself is confirmed, the specific remedy provisions of Chapter 5 beyond § 17200's own text were not separately fetched). **No distinct, synthetic-performer-specific civil penalty amount (unlike NY's express "$1,000 / $5,000") exists anywhere in § 17610's own text** — enforcement rides entirely on the pre-existing § 17500/17200 UCL framework.
- **§ 17610(g) — severability clause.**
- **No actual-knowledge qualifier found anywhere in the full captured § 17610 text** (confirmed by direct re-reading of all seven subdivisions) — independently re-confirms the prior discovery milestone's finding that California's duty, unlike NY's, does not condition liability on the duty-holder's actual knowledge of the synthetic performer's presence.
- **No AI-tool-provider carve-out or mention anywhere in the captured text** — § 17610(b)'s duty-holder is "any person," narrowed only by the conduct described ("create and cause to be published"); an AI tool provider is not named as a duty-holder or as an exempted class, the same scope-not-carve-out structure NY's § 396-b(3) presents.
- **No explicit operative-date clause found anywhere in the captured bill text** (SEC. 1, SEC. 2, or SEC. 3) — confirmed by a direct text search of the full stripped plain-text extraction for "urgency," "take effect," "operative," and "immediately": zero matches. This is a **Class A primary-evidence confirmation of an absence**, not an inference about what the absence means (see the effective-date analysis in the FGR itself for that separate inferential step).
- **California Constitution Art. IV, § 8(c)(1), independently fetched:** *"Except as provided in paragraphs (2) and (3) of this subdivision, a statute enacted at a regular session shall go into effect on January 1 next following a 90-day period from the date of enactment of the statute..."* § 8(d) defines an urgency statute as one with an express legislative "statement of facts constituting the necessity" passed by a two-thirds rollcall vote in each house — no such statement, vote record, or urgency designation appears anywhere in the captured SB 1050 bill text. SB 1050 is a 2025-2026 Regular Session bill (confirmed by the `bill_id=202520260SB1050` URL parameter and the bill-history page's own session heading).
- **§ 17500, independently re-fetched in full:** the pre-existing (1941, last amended 1998) general false-advertising misdemeanor § 17610(f) invokes by cross-reference.
- **§ 17200, independently re-fetched in full:** the pre-existing (1977, last amended 1992) unfair-competition definition and enforcement-chapter anchor § 17610(f) also invokes.

## Enactment/effective-date computation (independently performed, not inherited)

Enactment (chaptering/approval/filing, all three dated identically): **September 16, 2026** (confirmed by two independent primary pages, see above). No urgency clause (confirmed by absence in the primary text, see above) → Art. IV § 8(c)(1)'s regular-session default rule applies: effective date = January 1 next following a 90-day period from enactment. September 16, 2026 + 90 days = approximately December 15, 2026; "January 1 next following" that 90-day period = **January 1, 2027**. This computation rests on two independently fetched Class A primary sources (the bill's own enactment date and absence of an urgency clause; the Constitution's own default-timing rule) **combined through this review's own arithmetic/legal-mechanics application** — it is not itself a third sentence appearing verbatim in any single captured document.
