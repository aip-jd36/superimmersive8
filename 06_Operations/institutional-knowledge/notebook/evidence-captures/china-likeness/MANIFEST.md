## Load-bearing finding 1 — Civil Code Art. 1019 independently confirms the "information-technology forgery" language the discovery report claimed

Quoted verbatim from `court-gov-civilcode-personality-rights-fulltext_20260930T162743Z_16fb30a1.html`:

> 第一千零一十九条　任何组织或者个人不得以丑化、污损，或者利用信息技术手段伪造等方式侵害他人的肖像权。未经肖像权人同意，不得制作、使用、公开肖像权人的肖像，但是法律另有规定的除外。
>
> 未经肖像权人同意，肖像作品权利人不得以发表、复制、发行、出租、展览等方式使用或者公开肖像权人的肖像。

*("No organization or individual may infringe another's portrait right by defacing, defiling, or **using information technology means to forge [fabricate]**, among other methods. Without the consent of the portrait-right holder, no one may produce, use, or publicly disclose the portrait-right holder's portrait, except as otherwise provided by law. Without the consent of the portrait-right holder, the rights-holder of a portrait work [e.g. a photograph's copyright owner] may not use or publicly disclose the portrait-right holder's portrait by publication, reproduction, distribution, rental, exhibition, or other means.")*

**This directly, independently confirms the discovery report's claim** — Art. 1019 does contain explicit statutory language ("利用信息技术手段伪造" — "using information technology means to forge") covering technologically fabricated portraits, read from the actual captured statutory text, not accepted from the discovery report's characterization. Note also the second paragraph's distinct point: even a portrait **work's** copyright holder (e.g. a photographer) cannot use/disclose the depicted person's image without the depicted person's own separate consent — copyright in the artifact and portrait right in the person are treated as separate, non-substitutable rights. This is the same structural principle later confirmed, in an AI-specific context, by the Beijing Internet Court's official account (Load-bearing finding 4 below).

## Load-bearing finding 2 — Civil Code Art. 1023 confirms the exact portrait/voice relationship

Quoted verbatim, immediately following Art. 1022 and immediately preceding the start of Chapter 5 (Reputation and Honor Rights, Art. 1024):

> 第一千零二十三条　对姓名等的许可使用，参照适用肖像许可使用的有关规定。
>
> 对自然人声音的保护，参照适用肖像权保护的有关规定。

*("Art. 1023. Licensed use of a name, etc. shall apply, by reference, the relevant provisions on licensed use of a portrait. Protection of a natural person's voice shall apply, by reference, the relevant provisions on protection of portrait rights.")*

**Structural finding, confirmed directly from the raw text layout:** voice protection is not a separate chapter, not a fully merged identical right, and not an independent standalone provision — it is the **second paragraph of the final article (1023) of Chapter 4 (Portrait Right)**, applying the portrait-right protection provisions to voice **by reference/analogy** ("参照适用" — "apply by reference/analogously"). This is the precise mechanism: a **reference-application relationship**, not identity and not full independence. Chapter 5 begins immediately at Art. 1024, confirming Art. 1023 is Chapter 4's own closing article.

## Load-bearing finding 3 — no general commercial-use gate found anywhere in the captured portrait-right chapter

A direct text search across the full captured text of Arts. 1018–1023 (Chapter 4, Portrait Right) for "商业" (commercial), "广告" (advertising), "贸易" (trade), "营利" (for-profit), and "营业" (business) returned **zero matches**. The chapter's prohibitions (Art. 1019: unauthorized production/use/public disclosure) and its exceptions (Art. 1020: personal study/appreciation/teaching/research; unavoidable news reporting; state duties; public-environment display; public-interest/rights-protection) are framed entirely around **consent and enumerated exceptions**, with no commercial/non-commercial distinction anywhere in the operative text. **This directly confirms the check requested in this milestone's instructions:** unlike New York Civil Rights Law §§ 50–51's explicit "for advertising purposes, or for the purposes of trade" gate (see `LIKENESS-NY-SCENARIO-A-FGR-PACKAGE.md` for that statute's own captured text), Chinese portrait-right doctrine, on the text captured here, has no equivalent general commercial-use threshold — the prohibition is broader, and is narrowed only by Art. 1020's specific enumerated exceptions, none of which turns on commercial purpose.

## Load-bearing finding 5 — technology-neutrality check: directly supported by the primary sources

The task asked whether the primary sources actually support "portrait and voice rights are existing legal concepts; AI doesn't create a new category; synthetic generation affects application/evidence, not the underlying rule." **Answer, from the captured text directly: yes, on both ends of the pairing.**
- The Civil Code (2020, effective 2021) provisions (Arts. 1018–1023) say **nothing about production method or technology** anywhere in the captured text — Art. 1018's own definition of "portrait" ("肖像是通过影像、雕塑、绘画等方式在一定载体上所反映的特定自然人可以被识别的外部形象" — "the recognizable external image of a specific natural person reflected via imagery, sculpture, painting, or other means, on a given medium") is media/technology-agnostic by its own terms — "or other means" (等方式) leaves the list open-ended, and predates any AI-specific drafting by five years.
- The 2026 SPC Opinion's Article 4 does not purport to create a new "AI portrait right" or "AI voice right" — it applies the **existing** statutory vocabulary (姓名权/name right, 肖像权/portrait right, 声音权益/voice-related interests, 名誉权/reputation right, Art. 994 standing) to a new **fact pattern** (AI-generated/processed content), explicitly gated on the same **recognizability** ("可识别") threshold the Civil Code itself establishes.
This is independently reinforced by the Yin voice-case reasoning (Load-bearing finding 6 below), where the court's own stated holding explicitly frames the question as "whether AI-processed voice falls within the existing protection scope," not whether a new right exists.

## Load-bearing finding 6 — the 2024 Yin (殷某桢) voice-cloning case, in full

From `bjd-yin-voice-case-national-case-database-entry_20260930T160514Z_fd5321c2.html` (independently corroborated, same substantive text, by `thepaper-yin-voice-case-national-case-database-entry_20260930T160517Z_c7647561.html`):

- **Court:** Beijing Internet Court (北京互联网法院). **Case number (public):** (2023) 京0491民初12142号. **Judgment date:** 2024-04-23. **Procedural posture:** first-instance civil judgment; neither party appealed; the judgment is final/effective.
- **Parties:** Plaintiff 殷某桢 (Yin Mouzhen), a dubbing/voice actor (配音演员). Five defendants: Defendant 1 (a Beijing AI-tech company operating the platform where the TTS product was deployed via API, without further technical processing); Defendant 2 (a Beijing cultural-media company that had commissioned Yin's original 2019 voice recording and held copyright in the sound recording itself); Defendant 3 (a software company that used Yin's recording as sole training material to build an AI text-to-speech product); Defendant 4 (a Shanghai network-tech company operating the cloud platform where the TTS product was sold); Defendant 5 (a Beijing tech-development company that procured the TTS product for Defendant 1's use).
- **Relevant facts:** Defendant 2 provided Defendant 3 with Yin's recording under a Data Authorization Letter that was **not signed by Yin herself**. Defendant 3 used only Yin's voice as material to build the AI TTS product. At trial, courtroom technical demonstration (当庭勘验) of the product's output found the AI voice showed "high consistency" (高度一致性) with Yin's timbre, tone, and pronunciation style. The product had been played/used 3,256,728,530 times on Defendant 1's platform.
- **The three disputed issues, and the court's holding on each:**
  1. **Does voice-right protection extend to AI-processed voice?** Yes. The court's reasoning is built directly on Civil Code Art. 1023 para. 2, quoted by the court itself: *"对自然人声音的保护，参照适用肖像权保护的有关规定"* — and from this, the court holds that voice protection therefore inherits portrait-right protection's own **recognizability** precondition, defined as: "自然人声音的可识别性是指在他人反复多次或长期聆听的基础上，通过该声音特征能识别出特定自然人" ("a natural person's voice recognizability means that, based on others' repeated or long-term listening, that specific natural person can be identified through the voice's characteristics"). For AI-synthesized voice specifically, the court holds the determinative question is whether the specific natural person can *still* be identified — if the general public or relevant-field public can, based on timbre/tone/pronunciation style, associate the voice with that person, recognizability is established. Applied here: yes, established.
  2. **Was the use lawfully authorized?** No. Defendant 2's copyright in the **sound recording** did not include authority to license a third party's AI-ification of Yin's **voice itself** — these are treated as two separate rights. Since the Data Authorization Letter was not Yin's own signature, Defendants 2 and 3's use had no lawful basis.
  3. **Liability:** Defendants 2 and 3 (who AI-ified and used the voice without permission) are liable for damages; Defendants 1, 4, and 5 (who had no subjective fault — they did not know, and could not reasonably have known, the voice was unauthorized) are not liable for damages. Defendants 1 and 3 were ordered to issue a written apology. Yin's claim for emotional-distress damages was denied for lack of proof of severe harm.
- **Remedy:** Defendants 1 and 3 — written apology within 7 days. Defendants 2 and 3 — joint and several RMB 250,000 economic-loss compensation. All other claims dismissed.
- **Legal basis cited by the case database entry itself (关联索引):** Civil Code Arts. 995, 1000, 1019, 1023. (Arts. 995 and 1000 — general personality-right remedy provisions, read directly from the same already-captured Civil Code file, not a new source — establish, respectively, the victim's right to demand civil-liability remedies including apology, not subject to a limitations period, and the proportionality requirement for apology/reputation-restoration remedies.)
- **What this decision does NOT establish:** it does not purport to create a general statutory rule beyond its own recognizability-based test; it does not discuss or require any commercial-use threshold (consistent with Load-bearing finding 3); it establishes a **fault-based**, not strict, liability allocation (the three "innocent" defendants were not held liable); and it establishes that copyright ownership in a sound recording is legally **distinct from and does not confer** authority over the recorded person's own voice-related personality interest — the same separation-of-rights principle later confirmed, for portrait right specifically, by the Beijing AI-face-swap case (Load-bearing finding 4/7).

## Load-bearing finding 7 — the Beijing Internet Court AI-face-swap short-drama case: identification, findings, and unresolved claims

**Identification:** no case number or docket identifier was located in any source (official or secondary) for this case, despite the time-boxed search required by this milestone's own instructions. The strongest source obtained is the Beijing Internet Court's own official institutional account on The Paper (`bjinternetcourt-official-account-aiswap-shortdrama-case-explainer_20260930T160749Z_8bee52c9.html`), published (per its own embedded `pubTime` metadata) 2026-03-20 09:03, authored by two named Beijing Internet Court judges (赵琪, 高雅) of the court's Filing Division, with the court's own account self-referenced in-text.

**What this official source actually establishes (verified directly from its own text, not assumed from secondary reporting):**
- **Two related lawsuits, one panel:** the plaintiff sued Company A (production) and Company B (distribution/broadcast) in what this source itself describes as "两案" ("two cases"), heard together by one collegial panel ("法院就两案组成合议庭审理后查明").
- **Plaintiff identity: NOT established by this official source.** The court's own account refers to the plaintiff only as "国内某知名演员" ("a certain well-known domestic actress") — it does **not** name her. The name "迪丽热巴" (Dilireba) appears only in secondary media (multiple outlets — Sina, 163.com, China News Service, Tencent News, and others — all converging on the same name), never in the official court account captured here. **Per this milestone's instructions, her identity is recorded as media-attributed only, not court-confirmed.**
- **Facts:** Company A produced and published (via its own video account) a 44-episode, ~90-minute short drama; two segments used AI face-swap technology; the swapped face showed, in the court's own words, "**一定相似度**" ("a certain degree of similarity") to the plaintiff's portrait — **not** "高度相似"/"神似" ("highly similar"/"strikingly resembles"), the stronger framing used by secondary media (see Finding W below — a real, worth-preserving distinction, not force-reconciled). Multiple social-media platforms subsequently carried hashtag discussions questioning whether the plaintiff had appeared in the drama.
- **Company B's defense:** it held a lawful information-network-dissemination-right license for the drama, was not its producer, and had no subjective intent to infringe.
- **The court's reasoning on the copyright-vs-portrait-right relationship — directly on point, quoted verbatim:**

  > 涉案短剧中同时存在着肖像权与著作权的双重权利，而双重权利分属两个不同权利主体时，肖像权和著作权两种权利之间并不会互相吸收，基于两种权利之间的价值差异，应对人身权益给予更高保障的立法精神，对著作权的行使不能损及肖像权。因此，享有著作权授权许可并非侵犯肖像权的免责事由。B公司是否得到著作权人的授权并不影响对原告肖像权侵权的成立...

  *("The disputed short drama simultaneously involves dual rights — portrait right and copyright — and where these two rights belong to two different rights-holders, portrait right and copyright do not subsume each other; based on the difference in value between the two, and the legislative spirit of giving higher protection to personal/personality interests, the exercise of copyright may not impair portrait right. Therefore, holding a copyright license is not a defense to portrait-right infringement. Whether Company B obtained the copyright holder's authorization does not affect the establishment of portrait-right infringement against the plaintiff...")*

  **This independently, directly confirms — from the court's own official account, not from any secondary paraphrase — the discovery report's claim of a "separation between copyright licensing and portrait-right authorization."**
- **Company B's liability basis:** fault/negligence-based, not strict — the court reasoned that because the drama was short and the disputed portrait had "a certain degree of fame and recognizability" (一定知名度和辨识度), review would not have been difficult, so Company B's failure to review before publishing was a failure of reasonable care (未尽到相应审查和注意义务).
- **Remedy:** both companies ordered to publish a written apology on their own video accounts and pay compensation for economic loss (no specific amount stated in this source).
