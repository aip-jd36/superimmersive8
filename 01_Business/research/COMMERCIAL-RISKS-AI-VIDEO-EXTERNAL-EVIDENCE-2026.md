## Risk 1 — No clean chain of title → the deal stalls at legal review (most likely failure)

The dominant, near-certain consequence of no assurance isn't a lawsuit — it's that a sophisticated buyer's legal team won't approve deployment.

- **Akker Insurance (May 2026):** an AI-generated film "has no clean chain of title"; E&O is required by Netflix, Amazon, Apple, Disney+ and every major distributor (min. $1M/claim, $3M aggregate) as a condition of any distribution deal — "without it, your film cannot be distributed on any major platform." Underwriting requires a chain-of-title review documenting every element as originally created, licensed, or public domain.
- **Beverly Boy (2025):** "without a clean chain of title, most distributors will not even consider your film worthy of distribution."
- **Thoolie (entertainment lawyers, Jul 2026):** "You cannot build a distribution deal on rights you may not legally hold." Distributors ask one foundational question: "can you prove you own what you are selling?"

## Risk 2 — AI tool licenses may not permit commercial use; audio is the sharpest edge

- **Thoolie:** AI tool license agreements "vary significantly. Some grant broad rights… some restrict commercial use." A filmmaker must know what the licenses *actually* permit "not what you assume they permit," before distribution talks.
- **Rodriques Law (Nov 2025):** real scenario — a director used an AI voice tool to finish an actor's ADR; the E&O lawyer's question "who owns that sound?" and whether the actor gave written consent to be cloned "froze the process" with the distribution deadline looming.

## Risk 3 — The copyright may not exist → weak enforcement + false-warranty exposure

- **Thoolie / US Copyright Office / courts:** AI-only works "enter the public domain immediately upon creation. Anyone can copy them, redistribute them, or profit from them without the creator's permission and without liability." Settled law: Copyright Office (Jan 2025) → DC Circuit (Mar 2025) → Supreme Court declined cert in Thaler (Mar 2, 2026).
- **Consequence:** you can't stop others copying the AI-generated footage; and if you *warrant* full ownership you don't have, you've made a false warranty that shifts risk back to you ("producer warranties… shift risk to the production if that right turns out to be more limited than represented").
- **The prompt trap (directly relevant):** "entering prompts alone, even detailed and carefully crafted prompts, is not sufficient to constitute authorship." Only human *selection, arrangement, and modification* of outputs is protectable.

## Risk 4 — Training-data contamination you can't see

- **Akker:** "Generative AI models are trained on datasets that frequently include copyrighted works — images, audio, performances, dialogue — that were not licensed for commercial use."
- **Insurance Business America (2024):** live litigation (NYT v. OpenAI/Microsoft) drives "nervousness in the media industry about when it's okay to use AI… especially from an insurance perspective."
- This is unquantifiable residual risk — the honest posture is to *document and disclose* it, not claim to clear it.

## Risk 5 — Likeness / publicity / synthetic performers

- **SNR Films (Apr 2026):** standard E&O claims include "misappropriation of likeness… unauthorized use of music, name, or likeness."
- **Kelly Insurance (2025):** "name/likeness — a fictional character's name matches a real person who claims reputational harm."
- The Rodriques ADR voice-cloning case is a likeness/consent failure in practice.

## Risk 6 — The insurance gap makes the loss land on the producer

- **Akker:** with no established AI coverage, a loss "lands entirely on the production company, its investors, and potentially its distribution partners… That first major claim will reshape the market fast." Carriers are already writing AI exclusions — "the film that used AI to generate background crowd shots in three scenes will find its E&O application flagged." (Note: this is the *blended* live-action + few-AI-shots case — the ICP-2 scenario.)

## What the lawyers say to DO — which is exactly SI8's assessment scope

Thoolie's four recommended actions map 1:1 to SI8's domains:

1. **Document human creative contributions** — records of what was kept, discarded, modified, arranged (not just prompts). → SI8 Domain H
2. **Review AI tool license agreements** — know what commercial rights they actually grant. → SI8 Domain R
3. **Disclose AI use in copyright registration** — accurate scope of what's owned. → SI8 Domains H/D
4. **Build chain-of-title documentation from day one** — including AI tool licenses + which elements are human vs. AI. → SI8 core deliverable

Thoolie's close, usable as-is: *"Distributors are beginning to ask these questions. Having clean answers prepared before you enter distribution negotiations is significantly better than discovering the gaps under deadline pressure at the delivery stage."*

SNR Films' close: *"The cheapest time to solve a clearance issue is before the movie is released."*

## STRATEGIC REALITY — read before using any of this in sales

1. **These sources are written by E&O brokers and entertainment lawyers** — parties who sell adjacent risk-mitigation. The underlying legal facts (Thaler, NYT v. OpenAI, Copyright Office guidance) are real and verifiable; the *severity framing* is from incentivized authors. Use the facts; discount the alarm.
2. **The incumbent solution these articles point to is E&O insurance + a clearance attorney — NOT independent third-party assessment.** SI8 is entering an established category (clearance/E&O) dominated by lawyers and insurers. SI8's argument must be that a structured independent assessment is a *better or complementary front-end* to that process (faster, cheaper, standardized, an independent opinion a buyer can rely on) — not that the risks are new or that SI8 replaces counsel or insurance.
3. **This same evidence cuts at SI8's own methodology.** "Prompts alone ≠ authorship" is exactly why the ASSESS-001-style verdict (strong "Supports" on an authorship claim resting on a single self-attested prompt) was miscalibrated. The law says prompt-only authorship is weak — so an honest SI8 assessment of prompt-only work should reflect that, not stamp it clean. **This has since been addressed**: Reviewer Manual v0.2 (Jul 20, 2026) added the corroboration requirement to Domain H specifically because of this gap — this article is the external legal justification for that fix, worth citing if the calibration rule is ever questioned externally.
4. **This is the FEATURE-FILM / ICP-3 (Line Producer/EP) evidence base** — E&O, distributors, chain of title. Per the GTM decision (2026-07-19), that segment is deferred (wrong channel, wrong product shape, needs a different assessment object — see `ICP-DEFINITIONS.md`). This one-pager is therefore RESEARCH ammunition for a later ICP-3 track, not the Agency/Enterprise near-term motion. Don't let the strength of this evidence pull focus back onto a segment already deliberately paused.
